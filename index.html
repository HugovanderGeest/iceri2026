ICERI2026 abstract 477

Learn More with Generative AI

P. Lafeber, H. van der Geest · Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences (Netherlands)

Our students have found generative AI (genAI) solutions online already and experimented with it. Without guidance, this can lead to using shortcuts to prevent effort in their studies. This is human nature. It is our job as educators to teach them how to make use of these solutions in the best possible way; to enhance their work output after graduation but also to enhance their study results before graduation.

At the Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences (AUAS), we have developed a "Generative AI Study Coach" to do just that. We have researched the development of this working method with genAI and improved upon the first prototype to come up with a new prototype that actually achieves two things; students learn to study better, which makes them more independent and enables them as a student, and they can acquire more knowledge, which results in higher test scores from the same time investment.

This research is set up as a practice-based mixed-methods study with a quasi-experimental core. The HvA generative AI study coach is treated in it as a didactic prototype for a teaching method within the teaching practice of the HvA. The core of the design consists of a comparison between two conditions within existing first-year ICT classes: a condition in which students work with a study coach and a condition in which students use generative AI more openly. Because existing classes and regular lesson situations were used, this is not a fully randomised experiment.

The final measurement of 1 June makes a first comparison per specialisation possible. The picture is mixed, but positive in parts. For the Business, IT & Management (BIM) classes, the students' final scores were higher than the baseline from the beginning of the course. For the Fusion classes, the group with the study-coach prompt remained level, while the group without that prompt dropped in score. For the Cyber Security classes, students who used the coach scored higher than students who did not. Because of the small numbers and the difference in test difficulty for BIM, this remains a cautious statement. The strongest and most stable result is the better visibility of the learning process: more traceable thinking steps, clearer accountability and better checking of AI use.

Because of the classroom research and the need to treat students ethically while studying them, these outcomes can only be claimed with a large margin of error. It is not possible with this action learning method to come up with definitive test results. However, the students report positive outcomes of this study coach prototype and the critical feedback we have acquired was input for yet another version of the study coach that is now very promising. This version will be used at the AUAS as of the next semester, and interest has already been found in other institutions who would like to start using it too.

We found that transparent accountability and critical thinking do not stand apart from one another, but in fact strengthen one another. As soon as students have to show their prompts, intermediate steps, choices and checking of AI output more clearly, the chance decreases that thinking shifts invisibly to the tool. The study coach thereby helps above all to bound cognitive offloading better and to strengthen the assessability of the learning process, without a firm performance effect already being demonstrated on the basis of the current series of measurements.

Keywords: Generative artificial intelligence, online coach, higher education, self regulated learning.
